To be fair to the seal, you're the one who showed up. In that same study, dive logs covering 8,947 dives at three Antarctic sites recorded physical contact with a leopard seal on just four. The authors worked out about one interaction per two hundred dives, one physical contact per twenty-two hundred, and at most one injury per nine thousand, even assuming a quarter of all contacts caused one. And no curious seal went on to hunt or attack. Most of the time, it just wanted a look at you.
There's a gentler side, too. For months each year, male leopard seals sing underwater, for up to thirteen hours a day. A 2025 study of twenty-six males found they use the same five sounds in a different order for each seal, and that the songs are about as predictable as nursery rhymes. So the animal that swings penguins around by the head also sings.
And the seals have the bigger problem. They depend on sea ice for breeding and resting, and one assessment found their habitat along the western Antarctic Peninsula has shrunk by 21 to 28 percent. Krill declines and heavier krill fishing could also thin out their food supply. The last circumpolar surveys, in 1999 and 2000, counted more than 35,000, which researchers consider a substantial underestimate.
