It's March 2021, forty-five kilometers off Western Australia, and you're on a boat drifting above a deep underwater canyon. Two groups of orcas are circling. Then something enormous breaks the surface and exhales. A blue whale. The largest animal that has ever lived, an estimated sixteen meters long, nearly twice the length of the biggest orca in the water.

You'd expect a chase. It isn't one. The orcas take turns. Some swim alongside, some underneath, some drop back to rest and rejoin. Over the next few hours, they steer the whale out of the canyon toward the shallows, going for its jaw and its tongue. Just before three in the afternoon, the blue whale dies.

The orcas share the meal, and drive off a hammerhead shark and a few pilot whales hoping for a bite. No walk-ins.

Here's the part that doesn't add up. In the wild, there is no confirmed case of an orca killing a human being. So why would you never want one to notice you? Because the more you learn about how it hunts, the less that record feels like luck, and the more it feels like a decision.

First, the hardware. "Killer whale" originally meant a killer of whales, not people, and it's technically a dolphin, the way a tiger is technically a cat. A big male reaches about nine and a half meters and can weigh more than six tonnes, roughly four family cars. Its jaws hold forty to fifty-six curved teeth, each about ten centimeters long. And in short bursts, it can pass fifty kilometers an hour. That's a city speed limit, underwater, with teeth.

But the hardware isn't the problem. The software is.

You might assume an orca is an orca. It isn't. In the North Pacific alone, scientists describe populations that look almost identical and live completely different lives. Resident orcas eat fish, mostly salmon, chatter constantly, and each family keeps its own dialect. Bigg's orcas eat marine mammals, and they do something you wouldn't expect from the biggest hunter around: they go quiet.

Researchers who tagged mammal-eating orcas in Alaska found they stay nearly silent before a kill, and that they can find prey in almost total darkness, probably by listening for it. A predator that shuts up because its dinner has ears.

That silence is a tactic, not a limitation. The fish-eaters talk and echolocate constantly, because salmon don't care who's listening. Seals and porpoises do. One species, two playbooks, depending on who's on the menu.

And all of it is learned. A 2015 study found that female orcas lead their groups more often than males, and that older females lead most when salmon get scarce, as if experience is worth more than muscle. The knowledge doesn't die with the hunter. It's handed down. Try getting your own family to follow its elders like that.

Now watch what that knowledge turns into.

Say you're a seal, resting on a floating slab of ice off the Antarctic Peninsula. Researchers have watched what comes next. The orcas line up, sometimes seven side by side, and charge the floe together. The wave they make either washes you into the water or breaks the ice apart. Then they stop you climbing onto another slab, churning the water with their tails and blowing bubbles, until you're dragged down by your hind flippers and drown.

Switch bodies. You're a sea lion pup on a beach in Argentina, and for a few months each year, the shoreline itself is the hunting ground. Orcas approach swimming on their sides to hide their dorsal fins, ride a wave right up onto the sand, grab a pup, and wriggle back to deep water on the next wave. Experienced orcas succeed about half the time, and the young appear to learn it from their mothers.

Even the timing is planned. They hunt in the couple of hours either side of high tide, when the water is deep enough close to the beach to get in and out, and strong winds put them off, because bigger waves could leave them stranded too high on the sand. The orcas check the forecast. The pups don't.

And if you're hoping the ocean's most famous predator is at least safe, ask a great white. In 2009, orcas turned up at the Farallon Islands off California, where researchers were tracking great whites. Within eight hours, all seventeen tagged sharks were gone. Most didn't return until the following season, and the researchers concluded that a visit from orcas can keep great whites away for up to a year.

Off South Africa, two adult males nicknamed Port and Starboard killed at least eight great whites, eating the livers of almost all of them, and in some cases the hearts. Daily shark sightings at the local hotspot fell from more than six to about one within six months. The most famous predator in the sea checks for orcas before it unpacks.

Which brings us back to that canyon. In 2019 and 2021, researchers documented three blue whale kills by orcas off that stretch of Australia, reported as the first of their kind. The method was the same each time: chase the whale until it tires, then rotate. Small groups take turns biting while the others rest, until the whale is forced under and drowns. Two of the three victims were young, a calf and what looked like a year-old juvenile. The third was an apparently healthy adult.

Wave tricks. Beach tricks. Silent stalking. Shift work. None of that is a reflex. It's strategy, and it's passed down.

So where do you fit in? Once, as a business partner. Imagine you're a whaler in Twofold Bay, southeastern Australia, sometime between 1840 and 1930. A pod of orcas is helping you hunt baleen whales. They herd your targets toward the bay, sometimes swimming kilometers to alert you, and after the kill they take their cut. Your crew calls it the Law of the Tongue: the orcas eat the carcass's tongue and lips, and you tow the rest ashore. A male named Old Tom is the face of the pod.

That partnership didn't end with a fight. After a stranded whale was stabbed to death on a beach in 1901, the pod declined, and only seven of about thirty orcas returned the next year. By the mid-1920s, Old Tom had lost teeth gripping a tow rope. When he died in 1930, his body washed ashore with abscesses in his mouth, possibly from starvation, and the partnership ended with him.

Since then, the wild record is mostly near misses. In 1972, a surfer named Hans Kretschmer was bitten off California and needed around a hundred stitches. Experts think the orca mistook him for something else. A hundred stitches for a case of mistaken identity. A free diver in New Zealand was dragged under for more than forty seconds by an orca tangled in his line, which looks like an accident, not an attack. Those are the closest calls, and no fatal wild attack has been confirmed. In captivity, orcas have been involved in the deaths of trainers, and why is still debated.

If you sail off Spain or Portugal, you're in the one place where orcas and people keep colliding. Since May 2020, a small group of Iberian orcas has been circling sailboats and ramming their rudders. By recent counts, that's roughly seven hundred interactions, and by one estimate about a fifth of them left the boat disabled. The whole population is under forty animals, critically endangered since 2019, and only about fifteen of them are doing it. Fifteen animals, seven hundred encounters. Impressive work ethic.

Most of the time they leave once the rudder breaks. Picture being aboard: the orcas drift away, and you're the one left with a boat that won't steer. Reports differ on how many boats have actually sunk, but one went down off Lisbon in September 2025, and all five people aboard were rescued unharmed.

Scientists still argue about why. The International Whaling Commission says it looks more like play or socializing than an attack. One leading idea is that it started with a single orca and spread by imitation, the same way wave-washing and beach-stranding spread. Another suggests the boats are practice equipment for young orcas learning to hunt. What nobody has found is any sign that it's aimed at the people on board. Which is reassuring, and also a slightly strange thing to find reassuring.

So suppose an orca is heading straight for you in open water. Your options?

Swim away? It can pass fifty kilometers an hour. A champion sprint swimmer manages under ten. Climb onto a floating slab of ice? You just saw how that goes for a seal. Get in a boat? Ask the sailors off Gibraltar. Fight? Forty-odd teeth, six tonnes, and a species that has taken apart a blue whale.

Which leaves the least dramatic option, and the one the evidence supports: nothing. In the wild, orcas have left people alone, and nobody can fully explain why. Keep your distance, follow local wildlife rules, and let it decide you aren't interesting. Be boring. You've been training for this your whole life.

To be fair to the orcas, they have more reason to fear you than you have to fear them. A population of fewer than forty animals can't afford to pick fights with sailboats.

And what orcas do out there shapes everything around them. When two orcas cleared great whites out of one South African bay, smaller bronze whaler sharks moved into the role the great whites had held. Add or remove one top predator, and the whole food web rearranges itself around it. So no, you don't want an orca to notice you. But the ocean you're swimming in works better with them in it.

Go back to that canyon, March 2021. A blue whale surfaces and the orcas arrive. What's unsettling isn't the violence. It's the teamwork: patience, rotation, teaching, a shared meal at the end. That's what's looking at you when an orca looks at you. Not a monster. Something that plans, and has decided, so far, that you aren't worth the effort.

Let's hope it keeps that opinion.
